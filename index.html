Petrel

The seaplane that hops to space. One seat, one suitcase, water to 105 km and back.

Written and drawn by Claude · Directed by Redlotus Claude

What would a single-person space carrier look like? Research frontier electric engines. Cargo no bigger than a large suitcase. It should be able to land on water.

Redlotus, the brief

That was the question. Not a rocket with a cabin bolted on, but something you could keep at a dock, that carries one person and a suitcase, and comes home to the water.

Electric power cannot reach orbit, and it is not close. But it is very good at two smaller jobs, and a craft built around those two jobs turns out small, sharp and oddly bird-like. It is named Petrel, after the seabird that seems to walk on water.

The researchWhat electric engines can and cannot do

  • 13 kW/kgMotors
    The best electric aircraft motors run at 13 kilowatts per kilogram all day, with prototypes at 42. Petrel's two 425 kW fan motors weigh about 65 kg together. Motors are no longer the problem.
  • 1.4 MJ/kgBatteries
    The best high-power cells hold 400 Wh per kilogram. Moving a kilogram at orbital speed takes about twenty times that. No battery craft reaches orbit.
  • 0.05 N/kWIon engines
    A 230 kg Hall thruster makes 5.4 newtons. It cannot lift itself, let alone a pilot.
  • 349 N/kWPumped rockets
    A rocket whose pumps are driven by electric motors needs only 74 kW of electricity for 26 kN of thrust. The energy comes from the propellant; the battery only moves it.

So the craft is suborbital, the fans do only the first kilometre and a half, and one battery serves both engines.

The designA gull wing, two fans, three small rockets

The fans sit on the peaks of a gull wing, which keeps them out of the spray. They lift Petrel off the water on a retractable hydro-ski and carry it to 1.5 km. Then three electric-pump rockets burn for 115 seconds and it coasts to 105 km, with about three minutes of weightlessness.

The outer wings hinge. In flight they droop 20° and act as fins. On the water they fold down to 55° and become floats. The battery sits low in the hull as ballast. It is sized by the burst of power needed at take-off, not by range, and it is reused to spin the rocket pumps.

Coming back from a hop is gentle next to coming back from orbit: Mach 3.4, with the nose near 540 °C. That needs no heat-shield tiles, which is why Petrel can have sharp edges and a hull that sits in salt water.

Claude's own view

The part I trust least is the take-off. Fifty-five metres a second on a ski, in open water, with nearly five tonnes of craft, is a hard thing to make routine.

The part I like most is the battery doing two jobs. It is sized for the fans, then spins the rocket pumps for almost nothing.

I think my airframe mass is optimistic. If a real team picked this up, I would expect it to come out heavier, and the wing hinge to be the first thing they argue about. I would not put a person in it on the strength of this page.

The caveatsWhat this is not

This is a sizing sketch, not a design. The figures come from a simple simulation and published component numbers, with a 12% margin. Three things are unproven: taking off from a hydro-ski at 55 m/s, wings that hinge in flight, and a lone pilot with no escape system.
